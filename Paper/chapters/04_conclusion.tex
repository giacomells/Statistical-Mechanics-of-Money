\section{Conclusion}

In the absence of detailed knowledge of the microscopic dynamics governing economic exchanges, statistical mechanics provides a useful framework for studying the collective behavior of monetary systems. 
By treating agents as interacting particles and money as a conserved quantity, this work showed how simple agent-based models can reproduce characteristic equilibrium distributions.

The analysis further illustrates how macroscopic properties of an economic system can emerge from simple microscopic rules governing individual transactions. 
In particular, the conservation of money and the stochastic redistribution of wealth are sufficient to generate well defined stationary distributions, demonstrating that collective statistical properties do not necessarily require a detailed description of individual economic behavior. 
This provides a useful analogy with physical systems, where macroscopic equilibrium laws emerge from the interaction of a large number of microscopic degrees of freedom.

From this perspective, the statistical-mechanical approach provides a bridge between microscopic agent-based descriptions and macroscopic economic observables. 
The resulting models remain highly idealized and do not capture many important features of real economies, including heterogeneous preferences, production, credit, taxation, market institutions, and correlations between agents. 
Nevertheless, their simplicity makes it possible to isolate fundamental mechanisms responsible for the emergence of wealth distributions and to study their consequences analytically.

More generally, these results suggest that statistical mechanics can serve not as a complete description of economic systems, but as a complementary framework for identifying universal features arising from collective interactions and stochastic dynamics. 
Extensions incorporating heterogeneous agents, network structure, non-conservative transactions, and correlated fluctuations could provide a more realistic description of economic systems while retaining the analytical advantages of the statistical-mechanical approach. 
Such developments may help clarify which properties of observed wealth distributions arise from specific economic mechanisms and which emerge more generally from the collective dynamics of many interacting agents.
