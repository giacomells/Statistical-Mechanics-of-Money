\section{Results}

\subsection{Basic Money Distribution}

In this scenario, we consider a simple model of money distribution among agents without debt. 
The total money of the system is conserved, and no agent can have a negative balance.
The agents engage with each other randomly but always with a fixed amount of money in each transaction.

Said so, we can assume that the money distribution will converge to the Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution as predicted by the theoretical framework.
It is possible to see the results in Figure \ref{fig:NoDebtImage}, where the histogram of the money distribution is plotted along with the theoretical Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{NoDebtImage.png}
    \caption{The histogram shows the money distribution of agents after a $10^6$ number of transactions, while the red line represents the theoretical Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.
                The simulation was performed with $N=3500$, $M = 10 000$, and $\Delta m = 1$. Agents in this scenario can't have debt.}
    \label{fig:NoDebtImage}
\end{figure}

Later on it will be discussed how the richer agents don't really follow Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution, but they follow a power law distribution, as observed in real economies.
These agents are not in equilibrium with the rest of the system, therefore they are not part of the thermalized system.

\subsection{Money Distribution with Debt}
The situation now is similar but in this case the agents can have negative balances up to a maximal threshold debt~($md$).
As it is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:DebtImage}, after many interactions, the population seems to follow a Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{DebtImage.png}
    \caption{The histogram shows the money distribution of agents after a $10^6$ number of transactions, while the red line represents the theoretical Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.
                The simulation was performed with $N=3500$, $M = 10 000$, and $\Delta m = 1$. Agents in this scenario can have negative balances and the maximal debt is $md = 50$.}
    \label{fig:DebtImage}
\end{figure}

One way of limiting the total debt in the economy, as we explained in section \ref{sec:debtModel}, is the so-called required reserve ratio $r$~\cite{xi2005required}.
Every bank is required by law to set aside a fraction $r$ of the money deposited into the bank, and this reserved money cannot be loaned further.
To simulate this, we implement a two-pool monetary model in which wealth and debt are treated as separate conserved sectors. The population is split into two groups of size $N_+ = N/2$ and $N_- = N-N_+$, initialized with
\[
W_+ = \frac{M_0}{r}, \qquad W_- = \frac{M_0(1-r)}{r},
\]
so that the total net wealth is $W_+ - W_- = M_0$,

while the positive and debt pools remain separately conserved. At each step, one pool is chosen at random and a pair of agents is selected; a transfer
\[
\Delta = 
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{constant}\\
\eta \,\frac{x_i+x_j}{2}, & \text{fraction-pair}\\
\eta \,\bar{x}, & \text{fraction-system},
\end{cases}
\qquad 0\le \eta \le 1,
\]
is applied only if the payer can afford it. The output is then
\[
(x_1,\ldots,x_{N_+},-y_1,\ldots,-y_{N_-}),
\]
so that the positive side is represented by positive balances and the debt side by negative balances. 
This construction allows one to study a distribution with both positive and negative wealth without allowing direct transfers between the two sectors.
