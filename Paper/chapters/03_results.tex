\section{Results}
Different scenarios were studied and in the end a comparison between different models entropy was done with the same initial conditions.
\subsection{Basic Money Distribution}

In this scenario, we consider a simple model of money distribution among agents without debt. 
The total money of the system is conserved, and no agent can have a negative balance.
The agents engage with each other randomly but always with a fixed amount of money in each transaction.
It is possible to see the results in Figure \ref{fig:NoDebtImage}, where the histogram of the money distribution is plotted along with the theoretical Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{NoDebtImage.png}
    \caption{The histogram shows the money distribution of agents after a $10^6$ number of transactions, while the red line represents the theoretical Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.
                The simulation was performed with $N=3500$, $M = 10^5$, and $\Delta m = 1$. Agents in this scenario can't have debt.}
    \label{fig:NoDebtImage}
\end{figure}



\subsection{Money Distribution with Debt}

The situation now is similar but in this case the agents can have negative balances up to a maximal threshold debt~($md$).
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:DebtImage}, after many interactions, the population seems to follow a Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{DebtImage.png}
    \caption{The histogram shows the money distribution of agents after a $10^7$ number of transactions, while the red line represents the theoretical Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution.
                The simulation was performed with $N=3500$, $M = 10^4$, and $\Delta m = 1$. Agents in this scenario can have negative balances and the maximal debt is $md = 50$.}
    \label{fig:DebtImage}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Money Distribution with Proportional money transfers}
Let's now consider a scenario where agents exchange only a fraction $\gamma$ of their money.
Each agent can exchange only a fraction of their money, which is determined by the saving propensity.
The results of the simulation are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:SavingPropensityImage}, where the histogram of the money distribution is plotted along with the theoretical Gamma distribution.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{SavingPropensity.png}
    \caption{The histogram shows the money distribution of agents after $10^6$ number of transactions, while the green line represents the theoretical Gamma distribution and the red line represents the Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution with such temperature.
                The simulation was performed with $N=3500$, $M = 10^6$, and $\gamma = 1/3$. Agents in this scenario can exchange only a fraction of their money, determined by the saving propensity $\gamma$.}
    \label{fig:SavingPropensityImage}
\end{figure}

Another simulation was performed with multiple values of $\gamma$ to see how the distribution changes with different saving propensities. 
The results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:SavingPropensityMultipleImage}, where the 
number of agents N and the total money M are kept constant, while the saving propensity $\gamma$ is varied.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{ProportionalTransferDifferentGammas.png         }
    \caption{The histogram shows the stationary distributions for different values of $\gamma$.
                The simulations were performed with $N=3500$, $M = 10^6$, and $\gamma$ varying from 0.1 to 0.9. }
    \label{fig:SavingPropensityMultipleImage}
\end{figure} 
It is possible to see that as the saving propensity $\gamma$ increases, 
the distribution becomes exponential like, showing that if agents can exchange a larger fraction of their money, the distribution approaches the Boltzmann-Gibbs form.


\subsection{Stochastic process}
\label{sec:stochastic_processResults}
In this section, we present the results of simulating the stochastic process described in Eq.~\eqref{eq:wealth-growth}.
The process is defined by the stochastic differential equation~\ref{eq:ito-Relativewealth}.
The simulations runs $N=30{,}000$ trajectories, all initialized at $\tilde{w}_i(0)=1$, with $J=\sigma=0.5$ and time step $\Delta t=10^{-3}$ until $t=40$ (40{,}000 steps). At step $n$, the drift substep is integrated exactly,
\[
\tilde{w}_{i,n}^{*}=1+(\tilde{w}_{i,n}-1)e^{-J\Delta t},
\]
then the multiplicative-noise substep is applied exactly:
\[
\tilde{w}_{i,n+1}=\tilde{w}_{i,n}^{*}\exp\!\left(\sqrt{2\sigma^2\Delta t}\,Z_{i,n}-\sigma^2\Delta t\right),
\]
where the $Z_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ are independent standard normal variables. 
The terminal distribution was compared with the stationary inverse-gamma solution of the Fokker--Planck equation~\eqref{eq:17}, given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:18}, with $\alpha=1+J/\sigma^2$ and $\beta=J/\sigma^2$. Because trajectories are simulated independently, their sample mean is not constrained to equal one exactly.
In Fig.~\ref{fig:stochastic_process}, we show the histogram of the terminal distribution of $\tilde{w}$, along with the theoretical inverse-gamma distribution. 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{StochasticProcess.png}
    \caption{The histogram shows the stationary distribution of relative wealth $\tilde{w}$ after $N=30{,}000$ trajectories, while the orange line represents the theoretical inverse-gamma distribution.
                The simulation was performed with $J=\sigma=0.5$, $\Delta t=10^{-3}$, and $t=40$.}
    \label{fig:stochastic_process}
\end{figure}

In Fig.~\ref{fig:stochastic_process_multiple} is possible to see how the shape of the inverse-gamma like distribution changes with different values of $\sigma$ while keeping $J$ constant. 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{stochastic_process_multiple.png}
    \caption{Different shapes of the stationary distribution of relative wealth $\tilde{w}$ for different values of $\sigma$ while keeping $J$ constant. The simulation was performed with $J=0.5$.}
    \label{fig:stochastic_process_multiple}
\end{figure}
